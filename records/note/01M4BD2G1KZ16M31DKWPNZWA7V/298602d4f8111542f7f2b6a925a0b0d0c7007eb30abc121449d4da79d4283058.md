# 03 — Technical critique: attacking kernels A, B and C, and the hybrid they converge on

Iteration 3, technical track (see `STATE.md`). The brief is to attack, not summarise. Sources:
`01-failure-modes.md` (FM), `01-data-model.md` (DM), `01-prior-art.md` (PA), `01-ai-landscape.md`
(AI), `01-inventory.md` (INV), `02-kernel-a-patchbay.md` (A), `02-kernel-b-ledger.md` (B),
`02-kernel-c-namespace.md` (C), and sonic's `docs/product/patch-bay.md` (PB). The product critique
(front door, canvas UX, who the user is) is a separate document. This one leaves that ground
alone except where a technical fact decides it.

---

## Verdict first

1. **The self-scores are not comparable. Under one standard the three designs converge.** A
   scored 11/4/1 and B 12/4/0, but both gave themselves a Pass on isolation and capability for
   the same trusted, token-holding swarm process that C honestly marked Partial. Scored the same
   way: **A 6/9/1, B 6/10/0, C 8/8/0** (§1). C's lower self-score was mostly C being stricter
   with itself.
2. **Each kernel has at least one concrete break on the compositions it designed for:**
   - **A: the approve signal carries no content hash.** C2 merges "the latest cold candidate"
     (A §5 C2, step 5), so a round that lands between the human viewing a preview and clicking
     approve is merged unseen. Its agent-written decisions then become accepted basis through the
     relay (§2.1).
   - **B: an autosaved human edit is either signed with the human key, or it is not a lineage
     root.** If it is signed, the human key lives inside a module. If it is not, C3's "a human
     edit resets the bound" is gone (§3.1).
   - **C: the shipped C3 preset never tells the human that the budget stopped it.** It runs
     under `set -eu`, so the budget refusal (exit 75) kills the script before the inbox question
     is written (§4.2). C's C1 recipe also binds `kb/notes/` both read-only and as output, so
     every `make article` re-runs the KB step and the writer (§4.3).
3. **A and B share a scheduling bug that C does not have.** Both are push/level-triggered over
   runs that take minutes. Neither has a rule that says "wait while something upstream is still
   dirty or running". So the writer in C1 runs on an empty or partial claim set and spends its
   bound before research has finished (§2.4, §3.2). C avoids it because `make` pulls from the
   sink synchronously.
4. **The "A on top of a ledger in git files" hybrid is a relabelling, not a synthesis.** Put A
   on B and B's defining feature, consumer-owned subscriptions, is gone. What is left of B is the
   envelope and provenance stamping, which A §1 already had. Put it on git and you inherit C's
   storage. Not one of the breaks above lives at a layer boundary, so the hybrid fixes none of
   them. What it adds is three identity schemes, three authority mechanisms and two candidate
   sources of truth (§6).
5. **The cut that matters is by trust, not by data model.** My proposal, D, "attest at the
   edges" (§7):
   - **Kernel:** what must be unforgeable. That is a launcher that signs what a run actually read
     and wrote, a gateway that enforces spend against human-signed grants, and verifiers at the
     sinks (merge, publish, accept) that refuse anything without a signed chain back to a person
     who approved those exact hashes.
   - **Porcelain:** what may be wrong and must stay replaceable. That is wiring, scheduling and
     the graph, which is A's patch format, demoted from authority to plan.

   Scored by the same standard, D comes out at 7/9/0, below C. The argument for D is not
   the total. It is that D fixes five of the breaks found here inside the kernel: the approve race, relay laundering,
   loop and autosave spend, the open authority enum, and macOS input hashing (§7.4). The
   settle problem moves to the reference scheduler, where pull-from-sink fixes it. One break
   stays open: long-lived modules with private state (§2.5). D beats C only if a local
   gateway can meter swarm's subscription-login model traffic, which is unverified (§7.6,
   §8 Q3).

## 1. One standard, all three re-scored

**The standard.** A test is a Pass only if the design, as written, meets it for **swarm**, the
one module that exists, and not just for imagined modules. All three designs leave swarm as a
long-lived process holding the human token outside the sandbox:
- **A:** `swarm.merge` needs the token, or swarm change #3 (A §3).
- **B:** `swarm-adapter` is a daemon of ~300 lines of host code with grant
  `swarm:human-token:effector-only` (B §3.3). "Effector-only" is enforced by the adapter's own
  code.
- **C:** the swarm *server* (C §2, "Servers are the exception").

C scored tests 8 and 9 Partial for this. A and B scored Pass. One standard means one answer.

## 2. Kernel A: where it breaks

### 2.1 The approve race (human-gate bypass)

The rules as written:
- A §1, HUMAN: `approve` is a signal with no payload binding.
- A §5 C2, step 5: `merge` reads "the latest cold `candidate`".
- `rounds.reply` is "a person or another module" (A §3 descriptor), so it is not human-only.

Walk-through:
1. Round 2 finishes. Its preview and hand-back are in the inbox. The owner reads them.
2. A critic module the owner cabled to `rounds.reply` (legal, since `reply` is not human-only),
   or the owner's own earlier reply, triggers round 3. Round 3 writes snapshot s3 and two new
   proposed decisions that `supersede` round 2's.
3. The owner clicks approve on the round-2 view they are looking at. `owner.approve` → `merge`
   runs and reads the *latest* `candidate`, which is **s3**. Nobody has looked at it.
4. `merge` emits `merged` with `answers: <handback r3>`. The register accepts r3's decisions.
   They flow into `basis` on the next round of every feature, through a cable marked
   `gated: human`.

The gate held formally: a human-signed record is in the chain. But the human approved something
else. B closes this case (B §5 C2, step 6: `act swarm.approve {feature, snapshotSha}` plus the
`stale-approval` refusal). Swarm closes it too: D-014, approve merges exactly that sha. **A
regresses swarm's own invariant.** The fix is small: approve must carry the hashes it approves.
But it changes A's model. A signal stops being a bare happening and becomes an attestation over
content (§7).

### 2.2 Authority laundering through the relay

A's invariant is "`decision.status=accepted` needs a human-rooted signal in `derivedFrom`"
(A §1, RECORD). Two holes:

- **Nothing requires `derivedFrom ⊆ by.inputs`** in A's spec. B has this check (B §2.4) and A
  does not. A module could cite any human signal it knows the hash of.
- **Even with that check, one signal authorises any number of records.** The register legitimately
  receives one relayed `merged` and may then emit *any* set of accepted decisions citing it. "The
  register accepts exactly the proposed decisions whose `derivedFrom` is that hand-back"
  (A §5 C2) describes the register's behaviour. The kernel does not enforce it. If the register
  is ever an LLM curator (the KB track's natural evolution, INV implication 3), the gated cable
  carries content an agent chose under a human's signature.

The human signed "approve the merge". They did not sign "accept D-034 and D-035". B gets this
right: the approve batch contains the accepted revisions themselves, signed by the human (B §5 C2,
step 5).

### 2.3 The loop counter is either unbounded or needs B's machinery

A §1, LOOPS: "Any human-rooted input to a module on the cycle resets the count." "Human-rooted"
has no closure rule:
- For gated cables, A counts *accepted decisions* as human-rooted. They are rooted indirectly,
  through `derivedFrom`. So the definition is transitive.
- In C3 the writer's `brief` comes from `owner.say`. Every draft is derived from a human record.
  Under the transitive reading, every pass is human-rooted, so **the counter resets on every
  pass and `max: 3` never fires.**
- Under the strict reading (directly signed by the human module), gated cables stop accepting
  accepted decisions, and C2 breaks.

Each reading breaks one of the compositions. A third reading, "nearest human ancestor through
the trigger chain", is B's lineage root (B §2.4, step 5). That is a second provenance walk inside
A's scheduler, and more words in the spec.

### 2.4 The settle problem (wasted paid runs, wrong drafts)

A §5 C1, step 2 claims "depth-first from the sinks puts `research` → `kb` before `writer`… avoids
a wasted writer run". The SCHEDULE rule does not support that claim:

```text
run(m) iff reaches(m, sink) and dirty(m) and budget(m) > estimate(m) and m is not done
dirty = some HOT input's outlet has a revision m's last run did not use
```

1. `owner.say` gets r1. `research.brief` and `writer.brief` both get a new revision.
2. `writer` is dirty *now*. `kb` is **not** dirty, because none of its inputs has changed yet.
   Research takes about 20 minutes.
3. Depth-first order sorts one evaluation pass. It does not hold `writer` back until a module two
   hops up, which is still running, has finished. So `writer` runs on an empty claim set and
   spends money on a draft with no sources.
4. Research lands, `kb` runs, and `writer` runs again. The C1 preflight did not count this extra
   run.

This is the FRP "glitch" problem. Sonic never meets it because every module runs every 2.7 ms
quantum (PB, "Order and feedback"). When a quantum is a 20-minute paid run, the scheduler needs
one more rule: **m is not runnable while any ancestor of m (excluding back cables) is dirty or
running.** That is pull-from-sink, which `make` gives C for free.

### 2.5 Module-private state is a covert channel, so a cable is not a grant

A §4, "Where state lives", and A §7.7 admit that "module-private state is invisible to the kernel
by design". The consequence is worse than a gap in the probe:
- Patch X cables confidential client research into `kb.sources`.
- Patch Y, an unrelated swarm delivery, cables `kb.ask → rounds.lookup`.
- The KB is a long-lived MCP module whose index holds both. Swarm's agents can now retrieve
  patch X's material, although **no cable connects X to swarm.**

"No cable means no read" (A, stance) holds per *run*, not per *datum*. Every stateful module
(KB index, research crawl cache, swarm's worktrees) is a mixing point the patch cannot show.
This is the one break that none of A, B, C or D fixes in the kernel (§8, Q4).

### 2.6 A crash between merge and `merged`

`swarm.merge` has `failure: {retries: 0, then: escalate}` (A §3). Walk-through:
1. It runs `swarm approve`, and the branch is merged.
2. The process dies before `merged` is emitted.
3. The kernel escalates. The owner approves again. Swarm refuses, because the feature is already
   `done·merged`. `merge` emits `refused`.
4. `merged` never exists, so the register never accepts the decisions and `rounds` never gets
   `done`.

The decisions stay `proposed` for good, against a merged branch. B names this class of failure
(B §7.9: reconcile-on-start). A's spec has no reconcile rule for effects.

### 2.7 Adding a second critic to C3 (the "changer" persona)

In C3, wire `critic2.notes → writer.feedback` (`claim, many`: union, so `canConnect` accepts it),
again with `back: {max: 3}`.
- Each back cable keeps its own counter. The writer becomes dirty when *each* critic's notes
  land. If they finish 40 s apart, the writer runs **twice per pass**, and the second run
  re-revises a draft the first critic never saw.
- The preflight rule, "a counted loop's modules × (max+1)", does not say how to count two
  overlapping cycles that share a node. The real worst case is about 2× the shown figure.

This is §2.4 again, inside a loop.

### 2.8 Concepts a person must learn

| Persona | New concepts (A) |
|---|---|
| Runs a preset | patch, module, cable, inbox (human module), approve, budget, parked: **7** |
| Changes one (adds a critic) | + jack, kind (5), `canConnect`, fan-in rules per kind, doc-never-sums/mixer, hot/cold, back cable (counted / gated), loop reset: **≈15** |
| Writes a module | + descriptor, `agent`, `drivenBy`, `relays`, `sink`, `mode: value/service`, `many`, `poly`, run statuses (4), effects, conformance suite: **≈27** |

Hot/cold alone is, in A's own words, "where patch semantics become folklore" (A §7.6, citing Pd).

## 3. Kernel B: where it breaks

### 3.1 The autosave principal dilemma

B §4.4: the writing tool's working copy "appends a new revision with `prev`" on an explicit save
or after 30 s idle. B never says **which principal signs that append.**

- **Signed as the human.** The writing tool, a module that may also run agents (inline rewrite
  is the obvious first feature), must hold the human key, or be able to make it sign. That breaks
  P3 ("a key no sandbox holds"). It also turns every idle pause into a new lineage root with a
  fresh bound (B §2.4, step 5). Take an hour of editing with C3's critic on `debounce: 30s`:
  - up to about 120 lineages, each allowed the critic's `usd: 1.50` and the reviser's
    `usd: 3.00`;
  - **a ceiling near $540 per hour** for a composition whose author thinks it is capped at
    $4.50.
- **Signed as the writing-tool instance.** Human edits are no longer lineage roots. C3's escape,
  "the human edits d3 and saves d4: a new lineage root" (B §5 C3), stops working. The human cannot
  reset the loop by editing, only by a separate explicit act.

Either way, the idle-autosave interval and the critic's debounce are both 30 s. The critic fires
mid-thought, and the reviser forks the subject against the human's next autosave. That fork
blocks every `head: true` subscription (B §4.5) until the human resolves it. The design needs an
explicit, user-present act such as "submit revision", separate from "save working copy", and only
the act may carry human authority.

### 3.2 The settle problem, B's version

B §5 C1, step 4: the writer "dispatches once the extraction runs settle". The only mechanism is
`debounce: 60s`:
1. Nine `kb-extract` runs start. Seven finish within 30 s. One retries (`failure: retry 1`) and
   finishes at 3 minutes. One times out and retries, finishing at 6 minutes.
2. At 90 s, the writer runs on 7/9 of the claims (round 1 of 3). At 3½ min it runs on 8/9
   (round 2). At 6½ min it runs on 9/9 (round 3).
3. The owner adds one source by hand. It extracts, and the writer is **refused: bound** under
   root r2 (B §5 C1, step 7, which assumes a re-run is available).

So the bound designed to stop loops is spent on a race. The idempotency key (B §2.4, step 2)
treats every partial set as new work, correctly. B has no notion of "my upstream is quiescent".

### 3.3 The reads-set check is vacuous under broad reads

B's headline guarantee is "every draft footnote [links] to a claim it actually read"
(B §5 C1). But the writer queries *every* claim with `piece: article-1`, so they are all in the
reads-set. A writer that misstates claim c7 in paragraph 3, or writes an unsupported sentence and
footnotes any plausible claim, passes `cites ⊆ reads`. The check rules out only citing something
the run never loaded. That is a real guarantee against fabricated ids. It says nothing about
fabricated content. "Traceability is a property of the store" (B §5 C1, step 6) is true of
*access*, not of *support*. C at least checks that the quote occurs in the source blob (C §4).
B's kernel does not.

### 3.4 Schema evolution reaches the authority fields

B §4.3 makes atproto's rules kernel law, including "open unions, and `knownValues` instead of
enums". B §1's ACL says `accepted/admitted/approve ⇒ human principal`. The ACL is a deny-list of
three values, and the status field is open:
- Register v2 adds `adopted` (accepted with conditions) as a `knownValue`, and its fold treats
  `adopted` as in force.
- Nothing in the ACL lists `adopted`, so any instance principal may write it.
- An agent writes `status: adopted`, and the register projects it into `decisions.md` as in
  force.

Two independently versioned modules have opened an authority hole, and neither one broke a rule.
**Authority-bearing values must be a closed, kernel-owned enum with default-deny.** Additive
evolution is right for bodies and wrong for authority.

### 3.5 Offline and two devices: bounds and idempotency do not survive a merge

B §4.5 makes sync a G-Set union, with per-device segments. But dispatch decisions are local:
- Laptop and desktop are both offline, and both hold d0. Each dispatcher computes the same
  idempotency key, finds no `kernel.run.*` record *locally*, and runs the critic. That is two
  paid runs.
- On sync, there are two critique revisions with the same `prev`, which is a fork. The reviser
  refuses (`reason: fork`), and the loop stalls until a human picks one.
- Bounds counted per (sub, root) are counted per *replica*: N on each device, 2N in total.

Counters are not G-Sets. A bound that holds across replicas needs either a single online
authority for spending, or bounds split in advance per device (escrow). B has neither.

### 3.6 Two reactors on one subject

B admits ping-pong between two writers of one subject (B §7.6). The second-critic change (the
changer persona) triggers it directly. Both critics write subject `critique/writing/article-1`
(B §5 C3: "one subject per draft"), so their revisions fork each other on every pass. The fix,
per-subject `writers`, is a new envelope concept that is not in the one-page spec.

### 3.7 Concepts a person must learn

| Persona | New concepts (B) |
|---|---|
| Runs a preset | record, ledger, task (`admitted`), act, inbox, composition/`apply`, space: **7** |
| Changes one | + subscription (`on`, `reads`, `$trigger`), `each`/`batch`, `head`, labels/scope, subject/`prev`, fork, lineage root, bounds keying, `debounce`, `self`, derived graph and its diff: **≈19** |
| Writes a module | + envelope (9 fields), 6 kinds, principal classes, ACL, manifest writes/reads, the reads-set rule, ledger handle (4 tools), conformance, daemon/ingest runs: **≈30** |

The concepts that decide spend (`each` vs `batch`, how bounds are keyed) sit at the changer
level. That is where users will get them wrong (B §7.5 says so).

## 4. Kernel C: where it breaks

### 4.1 On macOS, the run file can misstate what the module read

C §2, Isolation: on macOS the view is "a scratch directory of symlinks to the bound paths". C §2,
Staleness: the run key and `inputs` hash "every file under every `ro` bind" *before* the run.
Symlinks point to live files, so:
- The owner has `kb/notes/crdt.md` open in Obsidian, and it autosaves at 0:40 into a three-minute
  `write` run. The module reads the new bytes. The run file pins the old blob sha.
- C §1 also never says whether "blob sha" means the working tree or HEAD.
  - If it means the working tree, an uncommitted edit gets a sha that never enters git history.
    The `path@sha` links that cite it can never resolve.
  - If it means HEAD, the module may have read different bytes from the ones pinned.

Either way, C's strongest property, run provenance "unforgeable by the module" (C §4), is only as
true as the filesystem stayed still. The fix is to **copy inputs into the view and hash the
copies**: materialise, don't link. That costs disk and time. A does this (`in/<jack>/<hash>`,
A §3).

### 4.2 The C3 preset never tells the human that the budget stopped it

C §5 C3 runs under `set -eu`. `ns run` "refuses with exit 75 if the next run's declared ceiling
would cross the budget" (C §5 C3, the bounds list).
1. The budget runs out before critic round 3.
2. `ns run critic` exits 75, and `set -e` ends the script.
3. The `ns put inbox/ …` heredoc never runs.

The human gets no question and no notice. The draft sits there. The same happens on any failed
run (exit 1). A bug in the reference preset is a bug in the human gate, because in C the gate
*is* the script. A kernel whose loop bound is "a convention" (C §5, "What the kernel cannot
see") has a failure path that is a convention too.

### 4.3 The C1 recipe re-runs the KB and the writer every time

C §5 C1:

```make
kb: claims
	ns run kb-file --bind claims=claims/$(SLUG)/:ro --bind notes=kb/notes/:ro \
	               --bind out=kb/notes/:out
```

`kb-file` reads `kb/notes/` and writes `kb/notes/`.
1. Its first run changes the blob shas of its own input.
2. The next `make article` computes a new run key, so `kb-file` is stale and runs again. It is an
   LLM merge, so it does not reproduce its output byte for byte.
3. The new notes invalidate `write`'s key (it binds `kb/notes/:ro`), so `write` runs again at
   about $1.

Every `make article` pays for a step nobody asked for. C §5 claims "Rerunning `make article`
re-runs only `trace`". That holds only if the human never re-runs before every LLM output happens
to be identical, which it will not be. This is an **undeclared feedback loop**: C's "feedback
reads the previous numbered version" rule exists, and this recipe does not use it. With
`ns watch kb/ -- make …` it becomes unbounded, capped only by `NS_BUDGET_USD` if someone set it.
Nothing marks a bind as a cycle, so `ns check` cannot catch it.

### 4.4 Numbered siblings can be spoofed, and the git index is shared

The C3 resume line `while [ -e "$D/v$((i+1)).md" ]` treats any file named `v4.md` as the writer's
output, whoever wrote it:
- a human save-as;
- an Obsidian template;
- a second recipe.

More generally, the human's own git use shares the working tree and index with `ns`. The
`.git/ns.lock` (C §2, Concurrency) covers `ns`'s move-and-commit. It does not cover the human's
`git add -A && git commit` or `git checkout` during a run. C §7.5 names "merge conflicts on
records" for concurrent human edits. The quieter failure is a human commit that sweeps up a
half-staged output, or a checkout that moves inputs out from under a run.

### 4.5 Sequential ids are not offline-safe

C §5 C2 names decisions `decisions/{proposed,accepted}/D-NNN.md`. Swarm and sonic already use
D-NNN (INV §1, pattern 8). Two devices, or two people, offline each mint D-034, and git sees a
path collision on merge. It is cosmetic in swarm's register today, because there is one writer.
In C the path is both the record's identity and its authority (protected path), so the collision
becomes structural. Use ULID or hash-based ids, and assign display numbers at projection.

### 4.6 The human key is used by an unsandboxed agent: the most likely bypass in practice

C §5 C2 is candid ("What does not stop it"), but understates how ordinary this is. The owner
works with Claude Code in their own repos, unsandboxed, as their own user. That is how this
research was written. If the commit-signing key sits in `ssh-agent` or the macOS keychain with no
per-use presence check:
- an agent in that shell can `git commit -S` directly under `decisions/accepted/`, without
  `ns approve`;
- `ns check` verifies the *signature*, not that `ns approve` ran;
- `ns approve` refusing to run without a TTY does not matter, because the agent never calls it.

The same applies to A's human-module key and to B's "OS keychain" human key: they are *readable
by no sandbox*, but *usable by any process running as the user*. **A signature proves the key was
used, not that a person meant it.** This is the hardest technical question (§8, Q1).

### 4.7 Concepts a person must learn

| Persona | New concepts (C) | Already known (owner) |
|---|---|---|
| Runs a preset | workspace, recipe, `ns run`, inbox file, `ns approve`, budget env: **6** | git, make, sh |
| Changes one (adds a critic) | + bind (`ro`/`out`), view, run cache key, numbered versions: **≈10** | sh loops |
| Writes a module | + manifest (3 modes), 4 kinds, envelope, protected paths, `ns check` fixtures, server `owns`/`emits`: **≈17** | JSONL, exit codes |

**For this owner**, C is the cheapest by a wide margin, because the rest of the count is tools
they already use daily. For a non-developer it is the most expensive, since make, sh and git *are*
concepts. That is the product track's call. Technically, the second-critic change is one
explicit line in `critique.sh` and has none of A's or B's double-fire behaviour. It is visible,
ordered and correct.

### 5.2 Walking a wrong article claim back to its source

**The case.** The published article says "Automerge 3 cut memory use tenfold". The source says
"up to 10× in one benchmark". Which hop introduced the error?

| Hop | A | B | C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is the root (source bytes) trustworthy? | The artifact is page bytes hashed by the research module. That is the module's word. | **Yes.** The fetch proxy records `{url, response sha}` in the reads-set (B §5 C1, step 2). | The source text is written by the research module. That is the module's word. |
| Source → claim | The claim body is free text with no quote. Diff it by reading. | The claim has a `quote`, but the kernel does not check it. | **Checked.** `trace` verifies the quote occurs in the blob (C §4). The error is localised: either the quote is exact and the claim *text* paraphrased it badly, or the quote fails and the claim stays `proposed`. |
| Claim → sentence | Footnote → claim hash. **But** the writer's `lookup` calls (the proxied `kb.ask`) are journaled *answers*, not claim records (A §3). The wrong phrasing may come from a KB summary the footnote does not point to. That is a second, hidden channel. | Footnote ∈ reads-set (vacuous, §3.3). `ledger why --para 3` gives the chain. | The footnote → claim@sha, and `trace.md` lists the row. |
| Uncited sentence | `citejudge` (LLM, partial) | `citecheck` heuristic (numbers, proper nouns) | Nothing beyond `trace`'s "lacks a chain" |

**No design has both a trusted root and a checked middle.** B has the root (the proxy) and C
has the middle (quote-in-blob). The right answer combines them: an **egress proxy that hashes
what was fetched, plus a deterministic quote check against those exact bytes.** That gives a
chain in which an LLM's words can be wrong only at hops a deterministic tool flags. D (§7) takes
both.

## 6. The emerging consensus, "A on top of a ledger stored as git files", attacked

B §7 ends "if A is chosen, steal B's ledger as A's substrate". C §7 ends "C is a storage and
capability layer that A or B could sit on". This reads like convergence, but each piece of the
stack has a problem.

**1. Two sources of truth.**
- B §1: "Current state… is always a fold over the log, never the source of truth." Files are
  projections.
- C §4: "Files are the source of truth, and git is the log."
- "A ledger stored as git files" has to pick one. When the owner edits `kb/notes/crdt.md` in
  Obsidian:
  - If the file is a projection, the edit is lost on the next projection, or it forks.
  - If the file is the truth, the "immutable ledger" now has a mutable layer, and B's
    provenance guarantees stop at it.
- Neither A nor B nor C answers this, because each assumed its own answer.

**2. Three or four identities.**
- B's `id = sha256(JCS)` plus `subject` plus `prev`.
- C's `path@blobsha` plus optional `id:`.
- A's outlet ref log per `(module, jack)`.

A link in the hybrid must be written in one of these forms. A git blob sha is not a JCS hash of
the same record (git hashes `blob <len>\0` + the bytes, after frontmatter formatting). So the
same record has two content addresses, and a verifier has to know which one a link means.

**3. Three authority mechanisms, and the weakest one governs.**
- A: cable rules (`drivenBy`, `relays`, `gated`).
- B: an ACL by principal class on `(kind, type, status)`.
- C: protected paths plus signed commits.

Layered, these must agree on every edge, or there is a path that one layer refuses and another
allows. Examples:
- A refuses `rounds → register.accept`. Does the storage layer also refuse an instance-principal
  append of `accepted`?
- C's protected path is enforced on *commit*. Does A's broker write through `ns`, or around it?

The hybrid inherits all three mechanisms. Its security is the *union of their holes*: §2.1,
§2.2, §3.1, §3.4 and §4.6 all survive.

**4. "A on B" deletes B.** B's contribution beyond storage is the consumer-owned subscription:
"adding a producer never touches a consumer" and late consumers by replay (B §2.3). Put A's
cables on top and subscriptions are gone. What remains of B is the envelope, content addressing
and kernel-stamped provenance. A already had all three (A, stance: "Like B, A has an immutable,
content-addressed record store with provenance stamped by the kernel"). **The hybrid is A plus
git storage.** Calling it a synthesis of three designs overstates it.

**5. Two schedulers, if B's dispatcher survives.** If the hybrid keeps B's dispatcher for
"dynamic" work (A §7.1 wants that for `poly`), there are two run rules, `reach ∧ dirty ∧ budget`
and `head-changed ∧ key ∉ done ∧ bounds`, with two bound mechanisms: per back cable, and per
lineage root. Then there are two places a loop can escape.

**6. None of the found breaks lives at a layer boundary.**

| Break | Owning layer |
|---|---|
| Approve race | A's signal semantics |
| Loop-reset ambiguity | A's scheduler |
| Settle | A's scheduler (and B's) |
| Autosave principal | B's principals |
| Open authority enum | B's evolution law |
| macOS view sha | C's runner |
| Covert channel through private state | all three |

Stacking layers adds no check that catches any of these. **The hybrid is a committee
compromise in the precise sense: it inherits every member's defects and resolves none of them.**

**What the consensus gets right.** All three agree on content-addressed immutable records,
kernel-stamped provenance, a thin envelope, explicit adapters for lossy coercion, atproto
evolution for bodies, human-only authority, and bounded loops. That agreement is real and should
be banked. The disagreement is about **what the kernel must *enforce* versus what it may merely
*describe***. Layering by data model (store under graph) does not settle it. Layering by trust
does.

## 7. The sharpest alternative: Kernel D, "attest at the edges; schedule anywhere"

### 7.1 The idea

> **Nothing irreversible happens without a verifiable chain of signed statements back to a
> person who approved those exact hashes. Everything in between is free.**

The kernel is not a runtime that mediates every flow. It is **three choke points and two
statement formats**. Wiring, scheduling and the graph are porcelain. They matter, but they are
allowed to be wrong, because being wrong cannot spend unapproved money or commit an unapproved
effect.

The prior art none of the iteration-1 tracks cite is **software supply-chain security**:
in-toto attestations, SLSA provenance and Sigstore. That field solved "provenance across
independently built, independently versioned tools that do not trust each other". It did not do
it by controlling the build graph. It had each step emit a signed statement, and checked the
chain at the deploy gate against a policy (an in-toto *layout*). CI systems differ wildly, and
the verifier does not care. That is exactly this platform's shape: modules differ wildly, and
the sinks (merge, publish, accept) are few and known.

### 7.2 The spec

```text
KERNEL D — ATTEST AT THE EDGES. Contract v1.

RECORD     A file: Markdown+frontmatter or JSON. Envelope {kind ∈ doc|claim|decision|artifact,
           type (namespaced, atproto law), id (ULID, stable), links{rel: [sha256]}}.
           Content address = sha256 of the file's canonical bytes (NOT the git blob sha).
           Stored in a git repo for history and transport; git is not the identity.

RUN STATEMENT    (in-toto Statement shape) signed by the LAUNCHER's key:
           subject = [sha256 of each output], predicate = {module@exec-digest, args,
           inputs = [sha256 of the copies the launcher materialised], egress = [{url, sha256}],
           spend = {usd, model, grant}, exit, at}. A module cannot write or sign one.

HUMAN STATEMENT  signed with a USER-PRESENCE key (Secure Enclave / passkey; one touch per act):
           approve{effect, exact hashes: snapshot, handback, accept:[decision sha256…]}
           grant{usd, scope (a composition id or record id), expires}
           A statement names hashes, never "the latest".

CHOKE POINTS (the only kernel runtime; none schedules anything)
  LAUNCHER  runs one module: materialises inputs as copies under in/, sandbox (nono/bwrap),
            no route out except the GATEWAY, collects out/, validates envelopes, signs the
            RUN STATEMENT. Refuses claim records whose quote is not in a cited input's bytes.
  GATEWAY   the only route to models and the network. Holds provider keys. Debits every call
            against a HUMAN grant named in the run token; refuses at zero. Hashes every
            fetched response into the run's egress list.
  VERIFIER  in front of every sink (merge, publish, accept, project-into-repo). Acts only if a
            SINK POLICY is satisfied: a chain of RUN and HUMAN statements from the effect's
            inputs back to an approve that names those exact hashes. Idempotent: checks the
            target's state first (already merged? already published?) and records the effect
            as a statement.

AUTHORITY  Closed, kernel-owned vocabulary: approve, accept, grant, redact. Default deny.
           A decision is "accepted" iff a HUMAN accept statement names its hash. Status
           fields in bodies are descriptive, never authoritative.
CONFORM    Launcher fixture suite per module; sink policies ship with their sink.
```

It is about 330 words, with **four new concepts for a module author**: record, run statement,
human statement and sink policy. A preset runner needs two: approve and grant.

### 7.3 What sits above it (porcelain, replaceable)

- **The patch.** A's YAML, kept as the composition format: plain data, diffable, the thing the
  landscape lacks (AI §3, gap 1). It is now a *plan*. A patch compiles to launcher invocations,
  per-module grant slices and sink policies. A wrong patch can waste a grant. It cannot cause an
  effect nobody approved.
- **The reference scheduler.** Pull-from-sink, with the settle rule from §2.4 (nothing runs
  while an ancestor is dirty or running), back edges reading the previous revision (PB's
  one-quantum rule), and iteration caps. `make` and `sh` remain valid schedulers (C). Swarm's own
  round loop is also a valid scheduler: swarm stays a module whose internal loop the kernel does
  not need to see, because its *effects* go through the verifier.
- **Views.** The derived graph from run statements (lineage, after the fact) and from the patch
  (plan, before the fact). Probes are file reads.

### 7.4 Against the breaks found above

| Break | Under D |
|---|---|
| Approve race (§2.1) | **Fixed in the kernel.** The approve names snapshot and hand-back hashes, and the merge verifier refuses a mismatch. |
| Relay laundering (§2.2) | **Fixed.** Accept names decision hashes. The register cannot accept anything else, and no relay module exists. |
| Loop counter / autosave lineage (§2.3, §3.1) | **Fixed for money, the thing that matters.** Spend draws on a human grant at the gateway. No lineage arithmetic resets it, and autosaves carry no authority because they are not presence-signed. Iteration caps are porcelain, and a bug there can only burn the grant. |
| Settle (§2.4, §3.2) | **Not a kernel matter.** The reference scheduler fixes it by pulling. A broken scheduler wastes a grant and never breaks a gate. |
| Vacuous reads-set (§3.3) | **Narrowed.** The launcher refuses claims whose quote is absent from the input bytes, and the egress hash anchors the root. Sentence → claim fidelity is still a judgement, as in every design. |
| Open authority enum (§3.4) | **Fixed.** The authority vocabulary is closed and lives in statements, not in body status fields. |
| macOS view sha (§4.1) | **Fixed.** Inputs are copies, and the copy's hash is what is signed. |
| Self-feeding bind (§4.3) | **Bounded.** The grant runs out. It is not detected. |
| Signing key used by an unsandboxed agent (§4.6) | **Mitigated.** A presence key requires a touch per act, so an agent in the owner's shell cannot produce one silently. |
| Private-state covert channel (§2.5) | **Not fixed** (§8, Q4). |
| Two devices (§3.5) | Spending needs the gateway. Offline spend draws on a per-device slice of the grant, cut in advance (escrow). Verification is offline-capable, since it is signatures over hashes. |
| Crash between effect and record (§2.6) | The verifier checks the target's state before acting and records the effect after. Restart reconciles. It is one implementation, in the kernel, not one per effector author. |

### 7.5 What D gives up, honestly

- **No kernel-checked graph.** `canConnect` becomes a lint on the patch, and the kernel does not
  refuse a "bad cable". D's position: a cable that cannot cause an unapproved effect or unbudgeted
  spend is a quality problem, not a safety problem. Lints are enough for quality problems. This
  is the claim to attack in iteration 4.
- **No kernel-guaranteed parking.** The scheduler parks. In knowledge work the human inbox
  reaches almost everything, so A's and B's "reaches a sink" rule rarely parks anything (both C1
  patches cable nearly every module to the inbox). The *grant* is the real cost brake, and D
  enforces it.
- **Pre-run budget sums are porcelain.** But the grant is a hard ceiling *before* the run, which
  is what test 16 is for.
- **Read access is still governed by the launcher's materialisation**, which follows the patch.
  A wrong patch can show a module data it should not see. Capability over *reads* is only as
  good as the plan. Capability over *effects and spend* is kernel-hard. If read confidentiality
  matters more than effect safety (client data), D must add a read policy at the launcher. That
  is the same complexity A puts in the broker.
- **Is D just C plus signatures?** Mostly in storage, yes, and that is deliberate: C won on data.
  The differences:
  - copies instead of symlinks;
  - the gateway as a hard spend choke point (C admits it has none, C §7.8);
  - content-bound, presence-signed human statements instead of signed commits on paths;
  - verifiers at sinks instead of `ns check` after the fact;
  - A's patch kept as the plan format.

### 7.6 Score (as designed, same standard as §1)

| Result | Tests |
|---|---|
| Pass | 1, 3, 4, 6, 11, 13, 15 |
| Partial | 2, 5 (the ceiling is the scheduler's), 7, 8 (swarm's server is still a long-lived trusted process; only its *effects* are gated), 9 (reads follow the plan, §7.5), 10, 12 (no live probe of a scheduler D does not own), 14, 16 (a hard ceiling only if the gateway can meter swarm's subscription-login traffic, Q3, which is unverified) |

**D: 7/9/0. That is below C's 8/8/0 on the totals, and D should not be argued from the total.**
The §1 standard counts swarm, and two of D's properties are unproven for swarm: the server's
isolation, and gateway metering of its traffic. The case for D rests on *where* it holds when
the walk-throughs are run, not on the count:
- the approve race, relay laundering, autosave spend, the open authority enum and macOS input
  hashing break A, B or C, and are closed by D's kernel (§7.4);
- D scores Partial on the graph, which A and B hold.

**D beats C only if the gateway can meter swarm's model traffic.** If it cannot, D's spend
guarantee shrinks to non-swarm modules, and D becomes C with content-bound approvals, which C
could adopt on its own. The cost: **D needs two services the owner does not have today**, a
model/egress gateway and presence-keyed signing. Swarm's sandbox and human token are the
nearest seeds (INV §1).

### 7.7 Why not "the kernel is only a protocol plus a conformance suite"?

That is the purest form of the Eurorack/LSP argument, and D is as close to it as the threats
allow:
- **A conformance suite tests a module once, at load.** It samples behaviour with fixtures. It
  cannot make a particular run's statement of what it read unforgeable. That needs a signer the
  module cannot impersonate: the launcher.
- **A spend cap needs a choke point that holds the provider keys.** A protocol can *describe*
  a budget. Only the process holding the keys can refuse the call: the gateway.
- **An effect needs someone to refuse it.** A protocol can define what a valid approval looks
  like. Something in front of `git merge` must check it: the verifier.

So D is the **minimum runtime the protocol-only idea needs**: three choke points that schedule
nothing, plus a protocol (two statement formats and an envelope) and a conformance suite.
Everything a pure protocol could do without enforcement, D leaves to the protocol.

## 8. The five hardest unresolved technical questions

**Q1. What exactly is human authority, and what does it bind?**
All three designs reduce it to "a key no sandbox can read". That fails against the owner's real
workflow: agents running unsandboxed as the user (§4.6). It also fails against content drift
(§2.1) and against autosave (§3.1).

*Best answer:*
- Authority is a statement signed with a **user-presence key**: Secure Enclave or a passkey,
  one touch per act, with no cached unlock.
- The statement **names exact content hashes**: the snapshot, the hand-back, each decision
  accepted.
- It is **verified at the sink that acts on it**.
- Saves, autosaves, replies and edits are *not* authority. They are content.
- The vocabulary is closed (`approve`, `accept`, `grant`, `redact`) and default-deny.

Swarm's bearer human token should become a verifier of such statements (A §3, change #3, but
content-bound).

**Q2. When does a downstream step run, given that runs take minutes and cost money?**
Push/level-triggering (A, B) causes glitches and wasted runs (§2.4, §3.2). Typo-level edits
cascade (A §7.8).

*Best answer:*
- Use **pull-from-sink with a settle rule.** A step is runnable only if a sink demands it,
  every non-back-edge ancestor is clean and idle, and a grant covers its ceiling.
- Dirtiness is *shown*, not acted on. Re-running a paid chain is a user or sink demand, never a
  side effect of saving.
- Deterministic $0 modules (trace, citecheck, projections) may auto-run, because their glitches
  are free.

That is `make`'s semantics plus PB's one-quantum back edge, and it belongs in the reference
scheduler, not the kernel.

**Q3. How is spend bounded across loops, fan-out, reset rules and devices?**
Per-cable counters (A), per-lineage counters (B) and per-path sums of self-reported cost (C)
are each defeated above: reset ambiguity, autosave roots, replica doubling and under-reporting.

*Best answer:*
- **Money is the bound. Counts are hints.**
- A human-signed grant (amount, scope, expiry) is debited by the **one gateway that holds the
  provider keys**, so a module cannot spend without it and cannot misreport.
- Fan-out draws on the same grant. Loops draw on the same grant. A reset requires a new grant,
  which takes a touch.
- Offline spend uses per-device slices cut in advance (escrow).
- Open sub-question: swarm uses a Claude *subscription login* (`claudeHome.ts`, INV §1). Can
  subscription traffic be metered at a local gateway at all, or only API-key traffic? This needs
  checking before D is chosen.

**Q4. How do long-lived, stateful modules (a KB index, swarm's server, a crawl cache) fit "a
cable is a grant"?**
No design answers this. The state of such a module is a mixing point between compositions
(§2.5). It is trusted by necessity (swarm's server holds the token), and a probe cannot see into
it.

*Best answer:*
- **Partition state by scope, and make the partition visible.** A stateful module is
  instantiated *per scope* (per space or composition), with its own state directory, unless its
  manifest declares `shared: true`.
- A shared stateful module is shown in every composition that uses it as a node joining them.
  Sink policies may refuse chains that pass through a shared stateful hop when the scopes'
  labels differ (for example a `client:` label).
- For the trusted servers (swarm), the server's agents stay sandboxed per round, as today. The
  server's *effects* go through the sink verifier. "Trusted process" then shrinks to "trusted
  not to leak between scopes", which is auditable only after the fact.

This is a real residual risk, and the synthesis should state it rather than design it away.

**Q5. What is the identity and source of truth when people edit files and modules append
records?**
B says the log, C says files, the hybrid says both (§6.1). There are three identity schemes
(§6.2), and sequential D-NNN ids collide offline (§4.5).

*Best answer:*
- **Separate the working copy from the record.**
  - Working copies (an Obsidian note, a draft being typed, swarm's feature JSON) are mutable,
    owned by one module, and outside the store.
  - A **record** is created only by a run through the launcher or by an explicit submit. It is
    immutable, and addressed by sha256 of canonical bytes, which is independent of git, so a
    record survives leaving git.
- **Identity:** ULIDs for the living subject. `D-034` is a display number assigned by the
  register's projection.
- **Git:** the transport and history for the record files, not their identity.
- Mutable projections (`decisions.md`) are regenerated and never edited back. A human edit to a
  projection is treated as a *proposal*: an adapter turns it into a proposed record, never a
  silent fork.

## What is the cable/jack/patch here

- **Cable.** In D, a cable is a line in a *plan*: "materialise this record set into that
  module's `in/<jack>`". It is a capability *for reads*, enforced by the launcher, only as good
  as the plan. The capability for *effects and spend* is not a cable at all. It is a signed
  human statement checked at the sink and the gateway. Sonic needs no such split, because a
  sound has no irreversible effect and costs nothing. Knowledge work has both, and that is where
  the patch-bay analogy has to stop carrying authority.
- **Jack.** A kind plus an optional type on a module's `in/` or `out/` subdirectory. `canConnect`
  is a lint.
- **Patch.** A's YAML, unchanged in form, demoted from authority to plan. Presets stay presets.
- **Quantum.** A launcher run. Back edges read the previous run's outputs by hash (PB's rule,
  which all three designs keep).
- **Parked.** A scheduler property. The grant, not reachability, is what makes "unwanted work
  costs nothing" true.

## Implications for the kernel (for iteration 4)

1. **Bank the consensus:** immutable content-addressed records, kernel-stamped provenance, thin
   envelope, explicit adapters, atproto law for *bodies*, human-only authority, bounded loops.
2. **Decide the cut by trust, not by data model.** Enforce in the kernel only what must be
   unforgeable: run statements, human statements, spend, sink effects. Everything else is
   porcelain and must stay replaceable. Swarm's own loop and `make` are both legitimate
   schedulers.
3. **Fix these regardless of which kernel wins:**
   - approve binds exact hashes;
   - the authority vocabulary is closed and default-deny;
   - authority acts require user presence;
   - inputs are materialised as copies;
   - the scheduler pulls and settles;
   - effects reconcile on restart;
   - subjects use ULIDs;
   - claims carry a quote checked against fetched bytes.
4. **State the residual risks plainly:** long-lived stateful modules (Q4), and sentence-level
   fidelity, which no design checks deterministically.
5. **Verify before deciding:** whether the gateway can meter subscription-login model traffic,
   and whether nono can deny a sandbox the use of the user's `ssh-agent` or keychain sockets
   (not just reads of the key files).
